How to use this

Every template below is grouped by who you're talking to, matching the categories in your CRM. Work down the Follow-Up Queue, find the section, personalize the brackets, send.

Three rules that make all of these work:

  • Name the context in the first line. "Good seeing you at the Chamber thing" kills the who is this? problem instantly. Generic openers get deleted.
  • Make "no" easy. Every message gives a graceful exit. Counterintuitively this raises reply rates — people answer when they don't feel trapped.
  • One ask per message. Never stack a check-in and a referral request together.

Never quote a rate, payment, or APR. The moment you put a number in writing you trigger Regulation Z disclosure requirements. Drive to a conversation instead.

1. Agents you know socially — the ones you'd stop and talk to

They know who you are and who you're with. So skip the introduction entirely — leading with it makes you sound like you're announcing yourself rather than asking for business. Go straight to the ask.

The goal isn't their whole book. It's to become the person they call when the normal lender says no.

Text — the opener

Hey [Name] — good seeing you at [place]. Reaching out on the business side: I'm working on growing my agent relationships this year and I'd like a shot at earning some of yours. Not asking you to move anything away from whoever you use. I just want to be the call when they're buried or a file won't fit — I shop multiple lenders, so the messy ones are where I'm most useful.

Derek

Those two commitments are the most persuasive lines in the message. Everyone claims to be responsive. Putting a same-day turnaround and proactive client updates in writing is a specific, checkable promise — and agents switch lenders over exactly those two things.

When they say "I already have a lender"

They will. This is the expected answer, not a rejection — and how you handle it decides whether you ever get a file.

Totally expected, and I'm not trying to replace them. Every agent should have a backup for when their main lender can't make something work, or is slammed and slow to answer. That's the slot I'm asking for. Costs you nothing until you need it.

The backup slot is winnable, and it's how nearly every lender-agent relationship actually starts. Their primary will eventually be unreachable on a Saturday, or will decline something. Be the person who picks up.

Offering something before you ask

If you want to move faster, lead with work instead of words:

Hey [Name] — got an open house coming up? Happy to put together a financing sheet for it: a few program options buyers can actually look at, co-branded with you. Costs you nothing and gives people something to take home. I can also sit the open house with you if you want someone there to handle the money questions.

Sitting an open house is the fastest way to earn an agent's trust. You're giving them free coverage and meeting buyers directly.

After a good conversation — lock in the next step

Great talking today [Name]. Two things I said I'd do: [X] and [Y]. I'll have both to you by [day]. And if anything comes up before then that needs a quick pre-qual, just text me — I answer fast, including weekends.

Response speed is the whole game with agents. It's the most common reason they switch lenders and the easiest thing to be excellent at immediately.

2. Agents you've lost touch with, or are genuinely close to

Same rule — they know who you are. No recap of where you work or what you do.

And the thing worth knowing about both groups: the reason they aren't already sending you business is almost never loyalty to another lender. It's that you never asked. That makes this far lower-stakes than it feels.

First, the honesty problem

If you haven't spoken to someone in a year and you turn up asking for referrals, they'll notice. You have two options and only one works: own it up front, or genuinely reconnect with no ask and circle back months later. Pretending it's a pure catch-up when it isn't is the version people resent.

Haven't seen them in a while — text

Hey [Name] — been a while since [the Elm St closing / we were both at the Board thing]. Hope the year's been good to you. Reaching out with an honest agenda: I'm working to grow my agent business and I'd like a shot at earning some of yours. Not asking you to move anything — just want to be on the list when your lender's slammed or a file's a mess. Would be good to catch up either way.

If you've actually closed a deal together

Your strongest possible opener, and most people never use it. They've seen your work — lead with that.

Hey [Name] — was thinking about the [Street] closing the other day. That one had some moving parts and you were great to work with. I'm building my agent business on purpose this year rather than by accident, and you're one of the first calls I'm making — mostly because you already know how I handle a file. Anything coming up I could help with?

Agents you're genuinely close with — drop the professional voice

With someone you actually know, the polished email is worse. It reads as fake and creates distance where you had none. Just say the thing.

Hey man, real talk — I'm trying to grow my agent business and I realized I've never actually asked you directly, which is kind of dumb on my part. Do you have room for another lender? Not trying to take over anything you've got going. Just want a shot at the next one. And if the answer's no, that's completely fine — I'd honestly rather know.

Alternate, shorter:

[Name] — quick one. Next pre-approval you need, can I take a run at it? I'd rather ask you straight than hint at it for six months. If you're locked in with someone, no problem at all, just tell me.

The single best question to ask a close agent

In person or on a call, this outperforms any pitch:

"Can I ask you something directly? I'm trying to grow the agent side of my business. What would I need to do to earn your next referral?"

It hands them the microphone and they'll tell you the exact criteria — turn times, weekend availability, how you talk to their clients, whether you'll sit an open house. That answer is worth more than any amount of guessing, and people rarely say no to someone who asked them how to earn it.

When they say "sure, send me something"

Don't let this evaporate. Convert it to a concrete next step within 24 hours.

Perfect. I'll send you two things by [day]: my pre-approval turn time in writing so you know what you're getting, and a co-branded financing sheet you can use at your next open house. And if something comes up before then, just text me — I answer fast, weekends included.

If there's no reply — one bump, then stop

Hey [Name] — no worries at all if now's not the time, just closing the loop on my end. Door's open whenever. Good luck with the rest of the year.

One follow-up. Then log them as a Prospect on the 30-day cadence and let the system bring them back around with something useful later. Chasing is what turns a reasonable ask into a nuisance.

3. People you casually know — making it known what you do

Someone from the gym, an old job, a friend of a friend. The goal is not a deal. It's to claim the slot in their head, so that in two years when their sister buys a house, your name surfaces.

Because of that: no reply is a fine outcome. Don't follow up, don't check if they saw it. The message did its job when they read it.

8. Active clients — the update that wins referrals

Weekly, even when there's nothing to report. Silence is the single biggest complaint borrowers have about lenders — and the agent is watching how you handle their client.

Hi [Name] — quick Friday check-in. [File's in underwriting / appraisal is ordered]. Nothing needed from you right now. Still tracking to close on [date]. I'll reach out the moment anything changes.

Send the same update to the agent on every active file. This is one of the strongest referral-relationship builders available to you and almost nobody does it.

10. In person

When it comes up naturally:

"I'm doing mortgages now — broker side, so I shop a bunch of lenders." [Then stop talking.]

They'll either ask a follow-up or change the subject. Both are fine. The mistake is filling the silence with your value proposition.

With an agent at an event:

"Who are you using for financing right now? … How are they on weekends?"

That second question finds the gap almost every time, and it lets them tell you the problem instead of you guessing at it.

11. Timing

  • Texts: Tuesday–Thursday, 10am–4pm. Avoid Monday morning and Friday afternoon.
  • Never before 8am or after 9pm. That's an actual TCPA rule, not etiquette.
  • Agents: mid-morning or early evening. Never Saturday — they're working.
  • Volume: five to ten personalized messages a week, not forty identical ones in a day. Word travels and identical texts get compared.

A note on "opportunities coming"

Be careful how you phrase this one. Saying or implying rates are going to drop is a prediction, and in writing from a licensed MLO that's the kind of thing compliance and regulators treat as misleading advertising. You can't know it and shouldn't say it.

Safe framing — talks about activity and seasonality rather than predicting the market:

  • "Things tend to pick up heading into spring."
  • "I'd rather have the relationship in place before it gets busy."
  • "There's more inventory coming on than there was six months ago."
  • "Worth knowing your options before you're under a deadline."

Avoid: "rates are coming down," "now's the time before rates move," "you'll be able to refinance next year."
